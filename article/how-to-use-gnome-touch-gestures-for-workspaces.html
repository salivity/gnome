How to Use GNOME Touch Gestures for Workspaces

GNOME integrates modern gesture support directly into its desktop environment, allowing users to navigate workspaces, view active applications, and access the application grid seamlessly. Powered by the Wayland display server protocol and the libinput library, GNOME delivers responsive, 1:1 tracked swipe interactions that mirror finger movements on both multi-touch trackpads and touchscreens.

The Foundation: Wayland and libinput

GNOME's gesture implementation relies on the Wayland compositor architecture rather than traditional X11. Under Wayland, the GNOME Shell compositor (Mutter) receives multi-touch events directly from libinput. This architecture eliminates the input latency common in older Linux display servers and enables true "1:1 gestures." In a 1:1 gesture, the UI elements physically track the speed, direction, and stopping point of the user's fingers, allowing actions to be cancelled or reversed mid-motion.

Three-Finger Touchpad Gestures

Starting with GNOME 40, the desktop environment transitioned to a horizontal workspace layout. On multi-touch laptop trackpads, three-finger gestures are the default mechanism for spatial navigation:

  • Horizontal Navigation: Swiping three fingers horizontally (left or right) shifts the active workspace. Swiping left moves to the workspace on the right, while swiping right returns to the workspace on the left.
  • Vertical Navigation (Overview Access): Swiping up with three fingers pulls open the Activities Overview, displaying window thumbnails and the top search bar. Swiping up a second time expands the full Application Grid. Swiping down with three fingers reverses this sequence, returning the desktop to its standard window view.

Four-Finger Touchscreen Gestures

While three-finger swipes dominate trackpad interactions, GNOME differentiates gestures on direct touchscreens to avoid conflicting with in-app multi-finger inputs (such as pinch-to-zoom or browser-based navigation):

  • Workspace Switching: Swiping horizontally with four fingers across a touchscreen transitions directly between horizontal workspaces.
  • Activities and App Grid: Swiping vertically up with four fingers opens the Activities Overview and the App Grid, while swiping down closes them.

Customization and Hardware Compatibility

GNOME's default gesture behavior is hardcoded into the shell to ensure system-wide consistency across various laptop vendors. However, users running GNOME on hardware that lacks official Wayland drivers can rely on third-party tools such as touchegg under X11, though these often lack fluid 1:1 tracking.

For users seeking deeper customization on Wayland—such as remapping workspace navigation specifically to four fingers on a touchpad instead of three—third-party GNOME Shell extensions, such as "Gesture Improvements," allow granular overrides of finger counts, swipe directions, and gesture speeds.
